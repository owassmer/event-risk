Markets come in at the second question: how likely each event is, once you have defined it. An event's probability is over the ledger's horizon, the one period every event shares, so that their probabilities and expected moves compare: for an event that could happen at any time, by the horizon's end; for one with a date of its own, on that date. In the store, an event is a proposition the portfolio watches. Markets rarely ask exactly that question, but those on a narrower or broader claim, a part of the event, or what drives it still tell you about it: logic turns some of them into bounds on it, and the conditional probabilities you state carry their prices to it. So break the event down: write the claims it implies, the narrower ones that imply it, its parts, and what drives it; and search for what markets ask. A market's price is what traders pay: their belief about the probability of what it asks, moved by the interest the money in a contract forgoes until the close and by the premium hedgers pay to shed a risk they hold. Each side is paid for in full until the close, so a long-dated price can sit nearer one half than the belief, by as much as the interest that money forgoes: an unlikely outcome's above it, a likely one's below. An outcome hedgers fear prices above its probability by the premium they pay. A market's depth is how much money stands at its quotes, and a book a single small order could move holds one trader's quote, however tight its spread.
